## Deciding who deals first

Before the first hand, both players cut the deck to see who deals. Everyone lifts a portion of the shuffled deck and turns up the bottom card of what they lifted. **Lowest card deals first**, and for this cut the ace counts as the lowest card of all (lower than 2). If there's a tie, cut again.

Dealing is a mild disadvantage in cribbage — the dealer's opponent gets first crack at pegging and counts their hand first in the show — so having to cut for it keeps things fair from the very first hand.

## Shuffle and cut, every hand

Before each deal, the dealer shuffles thoroughly, and then the other player (or, at a full table, the player to the dealer's right) cuts the deck. The dealer deals from the bottom half of that cut, restacks, and deals the cards one at a time, face down, starting with the player to their left and moving clockwise.

## How many cards go where

The number of cards dealt depends on how many people are playing:

| Players | Cards dealt to each hand | Extra card to the crib |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 6 cards | — |
| 3 | 5 cards | 1 card, dealt face down straight into the crib |
| 4 (2 vs 2 partners) | 5 cards | — |

In the three-player game, that single extra crib card is dealt before anyone discards, so the crib starts with one card already in it before the players add their own discards.

## Deal rotates to the left

After the first hand, there's no more cutting for deal — it simply passes to the player on the previous dealer's left, hand after hand, for the rest of the game. In a two-team game, deal still rotates seat by seat around the table (partner to partner to partner to partner), not straight across to your partner.

## Misdeals

If cards are dealt incorrectly — too many or too few end up in a hand, a card is exposed during the deal, or the deck wasn't properly cut — either player can call a **misdeal** before pegging begins. The cards are gathered up, reshuffled, and the same dealer deals again. Once pegging has started, small deal irregularities are generally let go; it's a table-side courtesy call, not a formal challenge.
