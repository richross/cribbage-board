## The basic rhythm

Once the starter is turned, pegging begins. The player to the dealer's left plays first, laying a card face up in front of them and calling out its value out loud. Play then moves clockwise, each player adding one card at a time to a shared **running count**, announcing the new total each time (for example, "seven... thirteen... twenty-one").

Card values for the count are simple: number cards are worth their face value, all face cards (Jack, Queen, King) are worth **10**, and the ace is worth **1**.

The golden rule of pegging: **the running count can never go over 31.** If the card in your hand would push the total past 31, you simply can't play it yet.

## Scoring while you play

Points during pegging are pegged the instant they happen — there's no waiting.

| What happens | Points |
|---|---|
| Your card brings the count to exactly 15 | 2 |
| Your card brings the count to exactly 31 | 2 |
| You play a card matching the rank of the card just played (a pair) | 2 |
| Three in a row of the same rank (pair royal) | 6 |
| Four in a row of the same rank (double pair royal) | 12 |
| Your card completes a run of 3 or more using the most recent cards | 1 per card in the run |
| Last card played before the count resets ("go" or 31) | 1 (unless it made 31 — see below) |

A **pair** only counts if the two matching cards were played back-to-back, with nothing else in between — the count has to be building consecutively. The same goes for pair royals and double pair royals: three or four of the same rank laid down one right after another.

**Runs** are a little different, and this trips people up at first: a run doesn't need to be played in ascending or descending order. It only needs the most recently played cards, taken as a group, to form a consecutive sequence of ranks. Order doesn't matter — sorted order does.

> **Example:** Cards are played in this order: 4♣, 6♠, 5♥. Look at the three most recent cards — 4, 6, 5. Sorted, that's 4-5-6, three ranks in a row. That's a **run of 3**, worth 3 points, even though 6 was played before 5.

A run can also keep growing as long as each new card extends the consecutive sequence.

> **Example, continued:** After that 4-6-5, the next player adds 3♦. Now the four most recent cards are 4, 6, 5, 3 — sorted, that's 3-4-5-6, a straight run of four consecutive ranks. That's now a **run of 4**, worth 4 points, replacing what would have been a run of 3.

## Stacking points on one card

A single card can score more than one way at once.

> **Example:** The count is at 10 (from a 4♣ and a 6♠ already down). A player lays 5♥. The count becomes 15 — that's 2 points for the fifteen. But that same 5♥, together with the 4♣ and 6♠ before it, also completes the 4-5-6 run described above — that's 3 more points. Total for that single card: **5 points** (2 for the fifteen, 3 for the run).

## The "go"

Sometimes nobody can play without breaking 31. If it's your turn and every card left in your hand would push the count over 31, you say **"go."** Play then passes to the next player, who keeps playing cards (if they can) until they, too, can't go any further without exceeding 31.

Whoever plays the **last card** before the count has to reset scores **1 point** for it — this is often just called "the go" or "last card." The one exception: if that final card brings the count to exactly 31, the player scores **2 points for the 31 instead, not an extra point for the go.** You get one or the other, never both.

After the count resets to 0, the player who played that last card does **not** lead the next series — the lead passes to the next player after them, going around the table in the usual direction, exactly as if a fresh round of pegging were starting.

> **Example:** The count sits at 26. It's your opponent's turn and their remaining cards would all push past 31, so they say "go." You still have a 3♥, so you play it, bringing the count to exactly 29 — no score yet. Now it's your opponent's turn again with nothing playable; they've already said go, so play returns to you if you have another card. Say you play a 2♦, hitting exactly 31 — you score **2 points for the 31.** The count resets to 0, and even though you played that last card, it's your opponent who leads the next series, since they're the next player around the table after you.

## Working through to the end

Pegging keeps going, card by card, count resetting to 0 each time it's needed, until everyone has played every card from their hand. The very last card of the entire pegging round always scores at least 1 point for whoever plays it (again, 2 instead if it happens to make 31) — so pegging never quite fizzles out without a final point on the board.
